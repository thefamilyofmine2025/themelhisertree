Immigration: Five million German-speaking immigrants came to the United States between 1800 and 1900. German immigration increased substantially during the 1830s and 1840s, when Germany was rocked by revolutions that aimed to unite the German states under a republican form of government like that of the United States. The failure of these revolutions caused thousands of Germans to immigrate to America. Many of these immigrants moved to the western frontier in the Ohio and Mississippi River valleys. Many German Americans opposed slavery and joined the Republican Party, especially under the leadership of Carl Schurz, a vibrant public speaker who campaigned on behalf of the Republican Party and the anti-slavery movement. German Americans also strongly opposed secession, the withdrawal of Southern states from the Union in 1860 and 1861. During the Civil War (1861-1865) German Americans constituted an estimated one-third of the 2.5 million Union troops.

Immigration: Alsace-Lorraine (German Elsass-Lothringen), historic frontier area of northeastern France, separated from Germany on the east by the Rhine River and drained by the Mosel River. The Vosges Mountains are in the east. Today Alsace-Lorraine consists of three departments: Bas-Rhin and Haut-Rhin, in the French administrative region of Alsace, and Moselle, part of the region of Lorraine. The chief cities are Strasbourg, Mulhouse, and Metz.

Immigration: The term Alsace-Lorraine was first used in 1871, when, by the terms of the Peace Treaty of Frankfurt concluding the Franco-Prussian War, the former provinces of Alsace and Lorraine, which had been under French rule since the middle of the 17th century, were annexed by Germany. They were returned to France in 1919, after World War I, by the terms of the Treaty of Versailles. During World War II, under terms of the armistice of 1940 between France and Germany, the territory was ceded to Germany, but France regained it after Germany's defeat in 1945.

Immigration: French is the dominant language in both Alsace and Lorraine. Alsatian, a German dialect, is also spoken in this area, chiefly in Alsace, although its use is decreasing. In many respects, the culture of the whole region is uniform, containing both French and German elements. At various times autonomy movements have been initiated, directed against Germany between 1871 and World War I and against France after World War I. The sentiment of the region during both world wars, however, was markedly pro-French.

BIOGRAPHY: John Melhiser's son, Alex, never married. Based on family tales, Mrs
Burton states Alex was a hard working boy at an early age. His stepmother,
Mary, was said to be rude, unkind, even mean to him. Quoting from Mrs
Burton's manuscript: "One day while working she became very angry with him
and hit him on the head with a hoe handle, breaking the handle of the hoe, which
is thought to have caused some brain damage. Then one day while riding a
horse, the horse threw him and dragged him for a distance, which also caused
some head injuries. Due to the two accidents he was sent to the Western State
Hospital in Hopkinsville, KY, a sanitarium for mental patients, where he spent the
rest of his life". In a letter Mrs Burton included in her manuscript, dated 28
August 1978, the hospital responded to her inquiry, with the facts that he was
admitted on 27January 1882 at age 23 years, with the diagnosis of
"melancholia", and that he was also "religious pre-occupied". He remained there
until his death due to lung failure on 20 January 1920.

BIOGRAPHY: FROM WILIAM BAUMANN

BIOGRAPHY: The land bordered on the east side of the Evansville, Owensboro &
Nashville Railroad (later acquired by the Illinois Central RR) that ran south along
Lewis Street, and was adjacent or close to the wooded area acquired in 1894
by the City of Owensboro and developed as Hickman Park (later re-named
Legion Park).
According to Mrs Burton, the Melhiser's initial house was a log cabin, not
unusual at that time in Daviess County. In any event, John and Mary Melhiser
apparently lived and farmed this property for over 25 years until his death in
1908. In the settlement of his estate in 1908, the property was divided among
the heirs of John and Mary (Daviess County Deed Book 85, pages 157 - 160).
Each of the six heirs received about 10-11 acres. This deed is described in
more detail in Chapter 2.0, The Baumann / Bauman Family.
In the decade 1880-1890 two of John Melhiser sons by his first wife were
married. William Henry married Josetta Bell Gibson in Daviess County on 12
July 1883. They would have five sons. John David married Josephine
Theobald in Daviess County on 20 July 1889. They had two children, but with
sad results. The first, a son, was born prematurely, and died at birth. Later a
daughter was born, but she lived only 8 or 9 months. . John's oldest son, Oswald
Alexander (Alex), never married.
Mrs Burton, in commenting on Mary Melhiser's personality, quotes older
family members who knew Mary. They state she was a stern woman with a fiery
temper. Mrs Burton quotes my aunt, Lillian Bauman, as saying her grandmother,
Mary, was the meanest woman she ever knew. Lillian was born in 1889 so
about 11 years old when Mary died, and so old enough to form impressions that
remained with her the rest of her life. Lillian said her grandmother was
especially mean to her stepson, Alex, but on the other hand favored her stepson
John David and her own son Herman. Lillian could recall John and Mary
Melhiser visiting their home (Al and Rosa Bauman, daughter Lillian and son
Charlie). John and Mary would speak to each other sometimes in German,
which none of the Baumans knew, and would make her parents very mad.
Whether this was done by John and Mary to conceal whatever they wanted to
say to each other, or was simply their reverting back to the language in which
they still felt more comfortable (after nearly forty years in this country !) is
unknown. Lillian commented that John Melhiser would, after all those years,
speak warmly of his native land and how he missed his family and friends there.
He was said to get very morose at times thinking back on it.
I know of only two photographs of Mary Melhiser. The first was a portrait
made of Mary and her first husband, Christian Heinrich Baumann and his
daughter, Lena, made shortly after their marriage in Columbus, Ohio. Based
on estimating Lena's age in the portrait, it was made shortly after their marriage in January 1855. That portrait is shown on page 36 of Chapter 2.0.
333. The second photograph is one made many years later and shows Mary
and John Melhiser visiting Mary's stepdaughter, Lena (Baumann) Peterson, and
her family in Evansville, IN. I estimate it to be about 1892 -1893 based on the
ages of the Peterson children.
